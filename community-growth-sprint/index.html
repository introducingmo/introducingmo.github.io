The problem

Attention isn’t the same as customers.

Most consumer brands don’t have an attention problem. They have a follow-through problem. People notice you, and then nothing gives them a reason to act, return or tell anyone.

  • Your posts get likes. Your tables don’t fill.
  • The promotion worked. Then it went quiet again.
  • You pay to bring people in. Every single time.
  • Customers buy once. Then they disappear.
  • Discounts are doing most of the selling.
  • Recommendations happen by luck, not by design.

None of this means your brand is weak. It means the attention you earn has nowhere to go.

The opportunity

People return to places where they belong.

A community isn’t a follower count. It’s a group of people with real reasons to show up: a tasting they want to attend, a class they look forward to, a launch they don’t want to miss, familiar faces they enjoy seeing.

Every one of those reasons is a visit. Every visit is a chance to sell. And people who feel part of something are more likely to come back, and to bring someone with them.

  • Attention into customers.
  • Customers into loyal fans.
  • Loyal fans into advocates.

The growth mechanism

Five steps. Each one leads to the next.

Customers move from noticing you to recommending you one step at a time. Community-led activations give them a reason to take each one.

  1. 01

    Attention

    They notice you.

    Give them something worth noticing: a story, a launch, an invitation.

  2. 02

    Visit

    They show up.

    Give them a reason with a date on it: a tasting, a workshop, a gathering.

  3. 03

    Purchase

    They buy.

    Make buying part of the experience: the menu, the product, the ticket.

  4. 04

    Return

    They come back.

    Give them a next time: a series, a membership, a familiar face.

  5. 05

    Recommend

    They bring someone.

    Give them something worth sharing, and an easy way to invite a friend.

And every recommendation becomes new attention.

Not everyone takes every step, and that’s normal. The aim is to make each step easier to take, and to lose fewer people between them.

AGORA’s first anniversary

Case 01AGORA CoffeeLebanon

A coffee shop people gather at, not just buy from.

The challenge
AGORA is a Lebanese specialty-coffee brand built around one idea: bringing people together. The challenge was making it more than a place to buy coffee, so people had reasons to visit, connect and take part.
The idea
Turn the café into a meeting point. Coffee workshops and tastings, cultural gatherings, talks and live music gave people reasons to come in beyond their usual order. For AGORA’s first anniversary, we gathered that community for an evening of coffee workshops and live performances.
Why people came
They were invited to something they valued: to learn about coffee, to enjoy the music, and to celebrate a place they felt part of.
What it encouraged
Visits, coffee and food orders on the spot, and orders for coffee machines and accessories that followed the event.
The lesson
When people value the experience, they show up in person. When they show up, they buy.
The line on launch day

Case 02IfranProduct launch, Beirut

A launch people lined up for.

500The line I planned for, when the advice was to plan for 100.
The challenge
A retail product launch needed more than online buzz. It needed people at the store, ready to buy.
The idea
Build anticipation before launch day, give people a reason to be there in person, and reward them with in-store offers. I set the goal at a 500-person line. The advice was to keep it to 100 to stay realistic. I kept it at 500.
Why people came
Anticipation that built before the day, the excitement of being there first, and offers they could only get in store.
What it encouraged
The 500-person line formed outside the store. People came in, and they bought.
The lesson
Launch attention turns into sales when you give it a place and a time to go.

Ticket orders

Case 03Matsuri FestivalBeirut, 2015

The first Japanese festival in Lebanon.

The challenge
Create Lebanon’s first Japanese festival, with no previous edition to build on, and get people to commit before the day.
The idea
Bring everything Japanese to one place: sushi, martial arts, calligraphy, anime cosplay and more. I organized the festival with members of the Japanese community, brought sponsors on board and gained the support of the Japanese Embassy. It took place on May 1, 2015, at 1866 Court & Suites, in front of the American University of Beirut.
Why people came
A first of its kind, a community to celebrate with, and a ticket that turned interest into a plan.
What it encouraged
Day passes sold online at $13.33 each, arriving as a steady stream of order confirmations in the days leading up to the festival, then crowds on the day.
The lesson
When an experience feels worth it, people commit in advance. A ticket turns “maybe” into a decision.
